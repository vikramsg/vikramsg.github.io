Not very long ago, my job used to look very different.
Of course there were parts of it that were not very different.
We divided worked in blocks of 2 weeks also called sprints.
Decided together what goes into a sprint.
Worked via opening pull requests.
Reviewed each others code.
But there were a distinct set of skills I built, and I am sure other engineers built that are not as relevant anymore.
For instance, selecting which libraries and frameworks to use could be make or break decisions.
In fact, job descriptions used to include knowledge of `numpy`, `scipy`, `pandas` etc, precisely because knowing these tools were very high leverage.
There used to be heated online discussions on things like `pandas` vs `polars` which often bled into the workplace.

These decisions don't matter anymore.
Not because some libraries are not better than others, in fact its generally quite the opposite.
However, that is not really the bottleneck anymore, because doing a migration like that is way easier.
If we figure out that the choice of our dataframe library is the current bottleneck, then one engineer alone can migrate to another desired library/framework within a week.
I know because I have done multiple such migrations in the past few months on substantial codebases.
`Gitlab` to `Github`.
`pip` to `uv`.
JSDom to HappyDom.
That is a big change, in more ways than one.
Because developers often deeply identified with their choice of language or tool or library.
Vim vs Emacs used to be a thing.
Or C/C++ vs Rust. Still is.
However now that these choices don't matter anymore, how do those of us who deeply identified with these choices deal with it? 

Actually there are many such aspects rapidly changing.
And one of the things I am trying to think about is the various ways our lives as software engineers are changing.
Because we have to first identify the `WHAT`. And then deal with the `HOW` (something I tell other software engineers in building software but is life so different?).

The second aspect that I am thinking about is adapting.
Chances are many of us went through similar learning curves.
There was the era of the internet as the web slowly took over the deskptop.
And people adapted by learning web technologies.
At almost the same time the cloud transition began and people adapted to that.
But all such transitions took many, many years and spread through slow diffusion.
There was space to specialize as a frontend, or backend or infrastructure engineer.
People could spend a long time, maybe their entire careers being the Java or Javascript or Ruby engineer.
