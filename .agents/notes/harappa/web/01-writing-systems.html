Step 1 · Foundation

The Big Split: Spoken Language vs. Direct Meaning

Before trying to translate Harappan signs, we have to ask whether they encode spoken words at all—or whether they work like road signs and clan badges.

Try it · Switch the reader's spoken language

Glottographic

Encodes a specific spoken language

DO NOT ENTER

Tied to English words. Change the language and the marks must completely change.

Semasiographic

Encodes meaning directly, no language required

Never changes. English, Hindi, and Japanese readers all get the same meaning without speaking the same sentence.

Why this matters for Harappa: Skeptics (Farmer, Sproat, Witzel) argue Indus seals are semasiographic—clan, god, or trade badges. Traditional scholars (Parpola, Mahadevan, Rao) argue they're glottographic. Next, let's see what glottographic scripts look like.
Step 2 · Glottographic ~20–40 signs

Alphabet: Every Sound Gets Its Own Sign

Alphabets break spoken words down into individual consonants and vowels. Because human languages only use a few dozen basic sounds, alphabets stay tiny.

Click any letter tile to inspect what it encodes
Sign-count clue: An alphabet almost never exceeds ~40 signs. Since the Harappan script has ~400+ distinct signs, virtually nobody thinks Harappan is an alphabet.
Step 3 · Glottographic ~20–30 signs

Abjad: Drop the Vowels, Keep the Consonants

Ancient Phoenician, Hebrew, and Arabic skip most vowels in everyday writing. Your brain fills them in automatically from context.

1 · Feel how an Abjad works in English
THE KING SAW THE FISH IN THE RIVER
2 · Real Arabic root: ك ت ب (k - t - b = "writing")
كتب → kataba ("he wrote") — Same 3 consonant letters; fluent readers infer the vowels from the sentence.
Sign-count clue: Even smaller than an alphabet (~22–28 letters). Again, way too small to match Harappa's ~400+ signs.
Step 4 · Glottographic ~35–50 base signs

Abugida: Consonants Carry a Default Vowel

Most South Asian scripts (Devanagari, Tamil, ancient Brahmi) are abugidas. Each consonant letter comes with a built-in "a" sound—small marks change or mute that vowel.

Click a vowel mark to modify the base consonant क (ka)
क
ka
Base consonant alone — carries the built-in short "a" vowel automatically.
Historical note: Later Indian scripts like Brahmi (3rd century BCE) are abugidas, appearing over 1,500 years after Harappa faded. Some Harappan signs do take small stroke modifiers—which makes scholars check whether they act like diacritics or separate signs.
Step 5 · Glottographic ~50–120 signs

Syllabary: One Whole Sign Per Syllable

In a syllabary, ka, ki, and ku don't share a base letter—every syllable gets its own completely unique symbol. That pushes the sign count up to ~60–100.

Click the syllable blocks in order to assemble a word
Decipherment milestone: Mycenaean Linear B (~87 syllabic signs) was cracked in 1952 without a Rosetta Stone because 87 signs was small enough to map into a grid of consonants × vowels. Harappa has 5× more signs than that.
Step 6 · Glottographic ~400–800 signs

Logo-syllabic Scripts & The Rebus Trick

How do ancient picture-systems write abstract words like "belief" or a person's name? They combine whole-word signs (logograms) with sound signs using the Rebus Principle: borrow a picture purely for its sound.

1 · The Rebus Trick in English
🐝 an insect ("bee")
+
🍃 a plant part ("leaf")
=
❓ just a bee and a leaf
2 · Famous Harappan Rebus Hypothesis (Asko Parpola — contested, not proven)
Literal pictogram: One of the most common Harappan signs clearly looks like a fish.
The big catch with Rebus: Rebus puns only work if you already know the exact spoken language. A pun in Proto-Dravidian (mīn = fish/star) doesn't work in Sanskrit or Munda. Without a Rosetta Stone, rebus guesses are very easy to overfit.
